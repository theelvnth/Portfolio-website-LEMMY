# Contextual Notes — Compiled

All locked notes from portfolio review session.

## TOP DOG

I made Top Dog as a portrait of earned dominance and what it costs.

The figure is a man in his early 30s. Dressed well. Seated deliberately. Aware of how he is being seen. His posture is controlled. He has arrived and knows it.

The suit, the chair, the gold stud earring—these are the face he presents. Status. Taste. Authority. That outward composure is the mask people are meant to read.

What sits over his face is something else. The skeletal form brings his inner state forward. Fatigue. Damage. Hunger. The emotions usually hidden are made visible here. The painting reverses the lie. What looks polished is the disguise. What looks distorted is the truth.

Behind him is a framed, shattered skull. Not decoration. A record. Like a hunter displaying proof of what was killed to survive. Success in this work is not clean. It leaves remains.

Top Dog is about power that was paid for.

## THE EXPRESSIONIST

I made The Expressionist after a moment that hit before I could think—brief, sudden, the kind that leaves you stunned before you understand why. I painted that pause between impact and explanation, before you can collect yourself and pretend you're fine.

The face in this work does not perform. Shock sits openly on it. Vulnerability is left as it is. I wasn't interested in understanding what happened. I was interested in holding what it felt like.

We tell ourselves our emotions are hidden. They rarely are.

The Expressionist is unpolished because the moment was. The vulnerability is the point.

---

## REBEL

I made Rebel about an obligation I believe we owe ourselves—the debt of being who we actually are.

When that debt goes unpaid, when you spend too long pretending or conforming, it doesn't just sit quietly. It turns inward. It becomes self-destruction. The conflict isn't always visible from outside, but it's constant.

The figure in this work carries that tension. Marked, worn, defiant. Not rebelling for show, but because the alternative is collapse.

Rebel is about the instances that try to confine you—and the refusal to let them.

## MESMERIZED

Some moments arrive without warning. They happen faster than comprehension allows—leaving you somewhere between awe and shock, lost in what just occurred.

Mesmerized was made for that instant. The rapid shift that takes time to register. The kind where past and present begin to collide.

A moment where composure breaks. Perception alters. And for an instant, nothing fully settles.

---

## ENCHANTEUR I

I made Enchanteur with a single intention: to create something beautiful enough to hold attention.

There was no narrative guiding the work. I painted the figure without a story to tell—just the gaze, left open for the viewer to meet.

While working, I realized the painting was doing something through its beauty. Not demanding attention, but quietly keeping it.

Enchanteur is about what happens when you stay with something long enough. The looking eventually turns inward.

---

## ENCHANTEUR II

Enchanteur II followed the first without a new intention, only a shift in presence.

I made this piece for what stays hidden.

The figure holds herself with composure. Nothing is offered forward. Nothing is explained.

Her gaze remains steady. It holds.

Enchanteur II is about elegance that guards what it took to arrive.

---

## LONGSHOT

I made Longshot from surviving a period where stopping would have been easier than continuing.

The subject holds composure, carrying what kept me in motion — the version of myself I had to become when the hours demanded it.

The struggle does not end. It continues.

Longshot is what remains after becoming what was required when there was no other choice.

## The Use of Skulls in My Work

Skulls appear in my work as a way to expose what the face alone cannot carry. They surface when composure, identity, or beauty is no longer enough to hold what lies beneath.

I use the skull to strip the subject back to what is internal and unresolved. It removes performance and leaves behind the weight of memory, pressure, and emotional consequence. In this way, the skull becomes a tool for clarity—revealing what would otherwise stay hidden.

This approach is not fixed across my practice. In works like Rebel, The Expressionist, and Mesmerized, the skull steps back, giving way to raw abstraction—gesture, distortion, and expressive marks carrying the emotional weight directly. When skulls do appear, it is by instinct rather than formula—when the image can't hold it any other way.

The skull is one of the ways I make visible what is endured rather than displayed. It sits between realism and expression, bringing interior weight to the surface without explanation or excess.

## Artist Statement

I make portraits that reveal what people carry beneath composure. What is presented to the world is often a facade.

I use skulls, abstraction, and layered compositions to expose what the surface alone cannot sustain. Many works exist in two states—masked and unmasked—showing both the surface and what lies beneath.

What concerns me is what is borne rather than performed. My work makes visible the strain, endurance, and quiet pressure held behind composure.

## Bio

Oluwadamilare Olalemi is a Lagos-based, self-taught digital portrait artist. He began working in realism in 2018 and, over time, realized that likeness alone was not enough. He wanted to go further—to reach what people hold behind the surface, not just the face they present. This shift led him toward a practice that blends precise rendering with abstraction, where portraiture becomes a way to show both what is seen and what lies beneath.

His work has been published in SuboArt Magazine (Lisbon, 2024) and has been part of several group exhibitions across Nigeria and the United States.
